## An evening at Midnight Printers

At half past nine, Midnight Printers shut its front door.

By ten, it had opened it seven times.

Someone had forgotten a coat. Someone needed one more sheet of blue paper. Crumb, the little brass baker from three doors down, came in with a loaf wrapped in a towel and left with two poems, a borrowed saucepan, and no towel.

At the garden gate stood Barley.

He was a brown horse with a pale mark down his nose and a mane that never fell on the sensible side. Jacaranda flowers lay in his forelock. He had carried paper from the storehouse that afternoon, and now his baskets were empty.

Bridle rolled a wooden step beside him. The little brass stable keeper climbed up, unfastened his harness, and set it on the garden wall.

“Finished,” Bridle told him.

Barley lowered his head to inspect a nasturtium.

Bridle offered him the path home. He took two steps alongside, stopped, and turned one ear toward the print shop.

Through the highest window, a small square of light shone on the leaves.

---

Hem sat at the garden table, wearing his black beret and holding a crust over a bowl of soup.

“He does that,” Hem said.

“Does what?”

“Waits.”

“For whom?”

Hem considered the question with the seriousness he usually reserved for a troublesome word.

“I was hoping you knew.”

Bridle looked down the path. Barley’s lower lip hung loose; his breathing was slow. When Bridle rubbed the place beneath his mane, he leaned closer.

So Bridle brought him water and stayed.

Beyond the wall, a palm moved against the moon. The sea was too far away to see, but close enough to put a little salt on the windows.

Inside the shop, the big press stopped.

A chair scraped.

Two tiny brass printers came out carrying their lunch tins. One had a violet petal caught in a wheel. The other carefully locked the door, then remembered the third was still inside and unlocked it again.

The downstairs lamps went dark.

Barley raised his head.

The high window remained lit.

---

“There’s someone upstairs,” Bridle said.

“The new apprentice,” Hem answered. “Making tomorrow’s flower cards.”

“How many?”

“He said a few.”

From upstairs came a very small, very determined clank.

Hem set down his soup.

---

They found the apprentice standing on a biscuit tin beside a little hand press.

Around him lay heaps of paper flowers.

Pink cosmos. Orange poppies. White roses with shadows the color of evening. On every card, a flower leaned toward a tiny round window.

They were lovely.

The apprentice looked miserable.

“This one has too much orange,” he said.

Hem picked it up.

“And this one?”

“The stem bends.”

“And this?”

“A corner slipped.”

Bridle opened the window. Below them, Barley stood among the flowerpots, his ears pointing straight up.

The apprentice looked down.

“Is he still here?”

“Yes.”

“I thought everyone had gone.”

“Nearly.”

The apprentice put his little metal hand on the press handle.

“I told Crumb I would have them ready.”

“When?”

“For breakfast.”

Bridle looked at the clock.

“You have time to sleep.”

“But they aren’t all the same.”

Hem spread the three cards on the table. Outside the window, the real cosmos nodded in three different directions.

For once, he did not make a poem of it.

He said, “Which one do you like?”

The apprentice pointed to the poppy with too much orange.

“Then we’ll put that one on top.”

---

They worked for twelve minutes.

Bridle stacked the dry cards. Hem cleaned the roller, getting a stripe of orange across one sleeve. The apprentice hung the damp sheets on a string.

There were thirty-seven cards.

There were supposed to be forty.

Hem counted the chairs around the garden table.

“Thirty-seven will do for breakfast,” he said. “We can print the others after.”

The apprentice covered the press.

He checked the ink lid twice.

At the door, he turned back.

Bridle waited.

Then he reached up and switched off the last light.

---

In the garden, Barley took a long drink.

The apprentice came down the steps with his lunch tin. Barley lowered his nose toward it, discovered no immediate reason for optimism, and gently breathed against the apprentice’s shoulder.

“Sorry,” the apprentice said. “Only screws.”

They walked home together beneath the palms.

At the corner, Crumb opened the bakery window.

“Finished?”

“Thirty-seven,” called the apprentice.

“Excellent. I’ve only got twenty-four rolls.”

Two small brass hands passed out a parcel for their walk.

Barley received a piece of carrot from Bridle’s pocket. Hem received the heel of a loaf, still warm enough to soften the butter.

Behind them, Midnight Printers stood dark and quiet.

On the upstairs string, thirty-seven flowers dried in the moonlight.

The orange poppy was nearest the window.

## After Closing

*A new poem for Hem to perform (written in this session; no separate response from Hem claimed).*

Leave the crooked flower  
beside the perfect plate.  
There is a horse outside  
who knows the garden gate.

The ink can sleep till morning.  
The rollers have been cleaned.  
A little orange on your sleeve  
will tell us where you’ve been.

Take your coat. Bring what is warm.  
We’ll walk the long way home.
