# Currency: a world of value

Editorial atlas for PointCast and University of El Segundo (UES). Research checked 5 October 2026. UES is the user's independent learning project; do not describe it as accredited or city-operated.

## Opening copy and editorial promise

Money is something people make together. It can be a weighed material, an issued object, an account balance, or a shared record. Its history connects everyday purchases with art, power, labor, technology, and belonging. Explore what people have counted, carried, promised, collected, and trusted, then use those histories to ask better questions about money's future.

This is an expanding atlas, not a claim to contain every historical currency, private token, or collectible. Its histories run alongside one another. A clay debt record, a shell, a coin, and a bank balance do not belong on a single ladder from “primitive” to “advanced.” The British Museum notes that cities and empires traded for more than 2,000 years without coins. A surviving Old Assyrian silver-loan tablet independently demonstrates sophisticated credit centuries before coinage. [1,2]

## Keep these distinctions visible

- Money: something used within a community to express prices, make payments, or carry purchasing power between moments. Particular forms can perform these functions differently.
- Currency: here, an identified monetary unit/system and its circulating forms. The directory must specify whether a record concerns a unit, an issuer, or a physical issue.
- Payment instrument or network: a way to instruct or move payment. A debit card, cheque, or Pix transaction is not a new national currency.
- Collectible: an object collected for historical, artistic, personal, or market interest. Its face value, material value, and collector price are different questions.
- Token: a context-dependent word. A transit token may buy one ride; a blockchain token is a digitally recorded asset whose rights depend on its design and terms.
- Legal tender: a jurisdiction-specific legal status, not a universal synonym for “accepted everywhere.” Never infer it from the appearance of a note or an object's title. [3,4]

## Twelve substantial learning modules

### 1. Before coins: keeping accounts
An obligation can outlast a conversation. Merchants at Kanesh recorded loans and repayment terms on clay, with witnesses and seals. One surviving tablet specifies six minas of silver owed, staged repayment, and interest if overdue. Begin here to distinguish the unit being counted, the material owed, and the record proving the obligation. The tablet is evidence of a loan, not automatically a spendable banknote. Activity: reconstruct its lender, borrowers, amount, dates, and witnesses. [2]

### 2. Materials become monetary
Shells, tea, metal, and cacao have served monetary purposes in particular settings. Portability, durability, recognizability, supply, and agreed units can matter, but usefulness is culturally situated. Cowries connect Asian, African, and oceanic histories; cacao adds a Mesoamerican example. Include trade power and enslavement alongside material ingenuity: cowries were used in European participation in African slave trading. Activity: compare three materials without declaring one inherently superior. [5,6,7]

### 3. Value can hold relationships
Not every valued object is best explained as shopping money. The Onondaga Nation describes wampum as living history that records agreements and conveys responsibilities and truth. Introduce Yapese rai through both monetary use and the object's community history. Ask what becomes invisible when a museum labels something merely “currency.” Activity: compare an exchange object, a ceremonial record, and an ownership record; do not reproduce sacred belts as classroom decoration. [8,9]

### 4. Coinage has many beginnings
Put a Lydian electrum piece, an Indian punch-marked coin, and Chinese bronze knife money next to each other. Their materials, shapes, and production methods differ. They invite questions about measurement, issuing authority, recognition, and authenticity. Early chronology is debated; use approximate dates and named regions, not one unqualified claim to the world's “first money.” Activity: inspect stamped marks versus cast shapes and explain what a user could actually verify. [10,11,12]

### 5. Money is a public image
Coins are small, repeated public messages. The 698–99 Syrian dinar carries religious text rather than a ruler's portrait, following a late-seventh-century reform. Images and inscriptions can assert authority, identity, and belonging. Modern collectors can read these objects as designed media as well as monetary instruments. Activity: annotate a coin's material, lettering, symbols, date, and authority; separate visible observation from historical interpretation. [13]

### 6. Paper, promises, and printing
A Ming note and an early Bank of England note tell different stories of paper money. The Met's Ming example is ink on mulberry-bark paper. A 1697 English running-cash-note fragment promised payment and recorded withdrawals. Paper reduces the need to carry heavy materials, but shifts attention toward the issuer, redemption, authentication, and public confidence. Activity: identify what each note promises, who makes the promise, and which details are absent. [14,15]

### 7. Global money has human costs
Follow Spanish-American silver beyond the pirate cliché. Pieces of eight circulated widely, while Potosí's mining and minting complex relied on coerced Indigenous labor under the colonial mita. A successful trading object can carry a history of extraction and violence. Activity: build a chain from mine to mint to merchant, recording whose work, risks, and authority made circulation possible. Avoid implying that every coin's precise silver source is known. [16,17]

### 8. When small change disappears
During the U.S. Civil War, coin shortages helped turn postage stamps into small-payment substitutes. Metal-and-mica cases protected fragile stamps. German Notgeld shifted between emergency payment use and collecting appeal. A 1933 Pismo Beach clamshell shows improvisation closer to El Segundo. Activity: design a fictional emergency voucher with a named issuer, use limits, expiry, and redemption rule; then stress-test what happens if the issuer fails. [18,19,20]

### 9. Stamps, editions, and collecting
The Penny Black, valid from 6 May 1840, prepaid a postal service. Its later collectible life is a separate story. Encased stamps show that an object's role can change with circumstances. Learn obverse/reverse, mint mark, denomination, condition, provenance, edition, and authenticity. Activity: catalog something already owned rather than buy a “rare” object. Record uncertainty. Age, a large printed number, or a low edition size alone does not establish market value. [18,21,22]

### 10. Most digital money isn't crypto
In modern banking, loans can create deposits; repayment can extinguish them. Banks remain constrained by capital, liquidity, regulation, and borrower demand. A debit card accesses or moves balances rather than constituting money by itself. M-PESA's Kenyan launch in March 2007 and Brazil's Pix launch in November 2020 show distinct digital-payment innovations. Activity: trace a payment through user, provider, account balance, payment instruction, and settlement. [3,23,24]

### 11. Blockchains: records, rules, and responsibility
A blockchain is a shared, tamper-evident ledger with rules for accepting updates. Bitcoin's design addresses double-spending without its proposed system relying on a trusted financial intermediary. That does not mean all trust disappears: software, governance, custody, and links to off-chain facts still matter. Tokens, stablecoins, bank deposits, and collectibles require different questions. Activity: simulate competing ledger entries and consensus with fictional balances; use no wallet, purchase, or real funds. [25,26]

### 12. Community art and possible futures
Nouns artwork is described by its project as public domain. That makes it a useful case for discussing shared visual culture, digital records, and community decision-making. It does not establish anything about a particular local print's issuer, rights, redemption, or affiliation. Frame Nouns Money prints/artchain and El Segundo as a local creative inquiry with documented objects supplied by their makers. Finish by testing several possible monetary futures rather than declaring a winner. [27,28,29]

## Twenty anchor objects and case-study cards

Each card should display classification, date/place, one supported observation, the question it opens, and a source link.

1. **Kanesh silver-loan tablet**, c. 20th–19th century BCE; credit record. Repayment schedule and witnesses survive. Question: how is a promise made durable? Met 66.245.17a; image explicitly Public Domain. [2]
2. **Cowrie shells**, Africa/Asia and wider networks; monetary material and cultural object. Question: how do trade routes alter supply and power? Do not assign the whole tradition one origin date. [5]
3. **Cacao beans**, Mesoamerica; commodity currency case. Smithsonian identifies monetary use among Maya and Aztec peoples. Question: can something consumable also circulate? Avoid unsupported modern-dollar conversions. [7]
4. **Wampum**, Haudenosaunee; agreement, responsibility, and living-history record. Question: whose description should lead the label? Center Onondaga testimony. [8]
5. **Rai stone ring**, Yap, museum example twentieth century; stone money. Question: how does collective knowledge support value? The example's date is not the tradition's origin. [6,9]
6. **Lydian lion electrum third-stater**, c. 575 BCE; coin. British Museum record gives 4.67 g and a 13 mm maximum diameter. Question: what does a stamp certify? [10]
7. **Indian silver punch-marked coins**, early regional and Mauryan issues; coinage. Multiple symbols were individually punched. Question: why needn't a coin be round or carry a portrait? Dating varies. [11]
8. **Qi knife money**, c. 350 BCE; cast bronze coin. British Museum example is 186 mm long. Question: what remains when a tool-shape becomes a monetary sign? [12]
9. **Syrian gold dinar**, 79 AH/698–99 CE; coin. Textual design follows ‘Abd al-Malik's reform. Question: what political or religious message travels with payment? Met 99.35.2386; Public Domain image. [13]
10. **Ming yi guan note**, Hongwu period, 1368–98; paper money. Met record specifies mulberry-bark paper; currently no image available there. Question: how is denomination visualized? [14]
11. **Potosí/Mexico City pieces of eight**, seventeenth-century examples; circulating silver coins. Question: how can circulation connect distant economies and unequal labor systems? [16,17]
12. **Hudson's Bay Company made-beaver tally**, museum date range 1670–1870; exchange record/token. “Made beaver” served as an account unit. Question: how did company-controlled exchange affect Indigenous autonomy? [30]
13. **Running cash note fragment**, England, 1697; redeemable paper promise. The £22 deposit was withdrawn in two payments. Question: where does a receipt end and circulating money begin? [15]
14. **Penny Black**, Britain, 1840; prepaid postage and later collectible. Question: what is the difference between paying for a service and buying general money? [21]
15. **Encased postage stamp**, United States, 1862; payment substitute. Gault's case protected a stamp and carried advertising. Question: can packaging change an object's use? [18]
16. **Notgeld**, Germany, 1914–24; emergency issues and collectibles. Their use and design changed over time. Question: can collecting demand change what an issuer produces? [19]
17. **One-dollar Pismo Beach clamshell**, 8 March 1933; local alternative money. Smithsonian names Restwell Cabins as maker. Question: what makes a local promise credible? [20]
18. **M-PESA / Pix comparison**, Kenya 2007 / Brazil 2020; digital services and payment infrastructure. Question: what changed for users without inventing a new national currency? [23,24]
19. **Bitcoin design paper**, 2008; proposed electronic-cash architecture. Question: how can a network reject conflicting spends? Read as a technical proposal, not an investment prospectus. [25]
20. **Nouns / local Nouns Money print**, contemporary; digital-art ecosystem and proposed local print case. Question: what exactly does the holder own? Publish local maker, edition, and artchain details only when documented. [27,28]

## UES learning studio: usable activities

**Start here, 15 minutes:** sort a coin, bank balance, debit card, postage stamp, souvenir token, and art print. Permit multiple classifications when context changes. Explain why a stamp normally prepays a service, and why 1862 is a special case.

**Object detective, 30 minutes:** choose one object; record material, dimensions, marks, issuer, use, provenance, and unknowns. Submit one observation and one interpretation separately. A source-backed uncertainty is a successful result.

**Trust lab, 30 minutes:** compare an IOU, a redeemable voucher, and a shared ledger. Introduce a missing record, failed issuer, or disputed transaction. Ask who can repair the problem and who bears the loss.

**El Segundo field notebook:** document a consenting participant's ordinary receipt, loyalty card, event token, or art print. Separate the printed denomination, purchase price, collector interest, and any documented redemption promise. Do not publish financial details, personal identifiers, or participant images without permission.

**Future studio, 45 minutes:** teams compare a cash-plus-instant-payments future, tokenized-bank-money future, and community-token future. Score accessibility, privacy, resilience, reversibility, governance, energy, and cost. Scores are arguments with evidence, not objective rankings.

Give each lesson an outcome, reading time, glossary, source notes, a check-yourself question with an explanation, and a printable worksheet. Completion marks are informal learning progress, not qualifications.

## Future-of-money framing

Present the BIS's 2026 case for tokenized central-bank and commercial-bank money as an institutional perspective, not consensus destiny. It acknowledges technical possibilities while raising stablecoin run, integrity, and sovereignty concerns. Ask what evidence would support or weaken each scenario. [29]

Keep current bank deposits, fast-payment rails, privately issued stablecoins, unbacked cryptoassets, NFTs, and prospective CBDC designs visibly distinct. “On-chain” is a technical property, not proof of backing, authenticity, copyright ownership, safety, or guaranteed liquidity. No price forecasts, buying prompts, or implied investment returns. [26,28,29]

## Registry, evidence, and image rules

Build the current/historical currency directory from SIX's official ISO 4217 lists, with source and snapshot date. List One contains currencies **and funds**, so preserve type distinctions. Model currency-to-jurisdiction as many-to-many. Bulgaria moved to EUR on 1 January 2026; BGN belongs in the historical view. Do not claim the registry exhausts private, ancient, or crypto units. [31]

Object metadata: title, issuer/maker, place/culture, date range, material, denomination/unit, functional classification, collection/accession ID, source, verification date, image credit/license, interpretation, uncertainty. Keep “source publication date,” “object date,” and “checked date” separate.

Prefer individually verified Met Public Domain or Smithsonian CC0 assets. Smithsonian metadata openness does not mean every associated photograph is reusable. British Museum CC BY-NC-SA applies only where marked and carries noncommercial limits. Link rather than copy uncleared imagery. Nouns' public-domain artwork statement does not automatically clear someone else's photograph, derivative print, branding, or implied endorsement. Respect culturally sensitive material beyond minimum copyright requirements. [27,32,33]
