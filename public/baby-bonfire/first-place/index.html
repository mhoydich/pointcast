First place

A place you can print: a tile with a ring where a piece lands, a puck that drops in beside it, and a recess underneath for a tap tag. It is the first Baby Bonfire object with files someone could make.

Nobody has printed or built this yet. It is a first draft for makers to try and correct. The shapes have been checked by computer and by nothing else.

This place holds no flame. Keep printed plastic away from candles, stoves and anything hot. PLA goes soft at about 60 °C and PETG at about 80 °C: one filament maker's data sheets give a softening temperature of 63 °C for its PLA and 84 °C for its PETG. Both are far below the heat of a candle flame or a stove.

Not a product. Nothing is for sale. No health claims are made for it.

What this page proposes

The open spec says a place can be a palm-size tile, that it has a tap tag, and that it may hold a swappable puck. It leaves the dimensions and the way a puck seats undecided.

This page fills those gaps with one set of numbers so that there is something to print and argue with. Every number here is a v0.1 proposal. It does not change the spec.

In The Shelf sketch the puck sits under the stone. Here it drops in from the top, so the tile prints without supports and a sensing puck can see the room.

The parts and the files

  • Part 1

    Place tile

    The place itself. A ring on top marks where a piece lands, and the recess for the tag is under the middle of that ring. Beside it is a round bay with a hole through it for a puck. Four small feet.

    100 × 100 × 9.2 mm. 2,720 triangles, 133 KB. Prints: top face down. The feet and the tag recess face up while it prints.

    place-tile.stl

  • Part 2

    Blank puck

    Fills the bay of a place that does no sensing. A place with no puck at all is still a place.

    46 × 46 × 6.3 mm. 764 triangles, 37 KB. Prints: chamfered side down.

    puck-blank.stl

  • Part 3 · optional

    Sensing puck shell

    Optional and untested. A cup that fits the same bay, with room inside for a small board.

    46 × 46 × 24 mm. 956 triangles, 47 KB. Prints: chamfered side down, open end up.

    puck-shell.stl

  • Part 4 · optional

    Vented lid

    Optional and untested. Pushes onto the shell. Slots let air and light in.

    46 × 46 × 4.5 mm. 2,140 triangles, 105 KB. Prints: flat face down, lip up.

    puck-lid.stl

params.json manifest.json

The files are binary STL in millimetres, and each one sits the way it prints. They are written by one script with no dependencies from one file of numbers, so anyone with Node can change a number and make them again: node scripts/baby-bonfire/first-place.mjs.

Putting it together

  1. Print the tile and the blank puck. Neither needs supports.
  2. Turn the tile feet up. Press a round 25 mm tag sticker into the shallow recess, in the middle.
  3. Turn the tile over onto its feet. The ring on top is now directly above the tag. That ring is where a piece lands.
  4. Drop the puck into the bay, chamfered side down. It should stand about 1 mm proud of the tile and turn freely. To take it out, lift the tile and push the puck up from below.
  5. Tap a phone on the ring to see whether it reads the tag through the tile. Nobody has tried this yet.

That is the whole of this first place: a marked spot with a tag, and a seat for a puck. It needs no power.

Printing: starting points

  • Material. PLA or PETG. PETG holds its shape to a higher temperature. Neither belongs near heat.
  • Settings. A 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, three walls, four solid layers top and bottom, 15% infill. Use the temperatures printed on your spool.
  • No supports, no brim. Sitting the way the files sit, no face overhangs by more than 45° and nothing bridges.
  • The tile prints top face down. Its top takes the finish of your print bed. The chamfer round that face gives the first layer somewhere to spread.
  • Bed. The tile is 100 mm square and each puck part is 46 mm across, so all four parts fit together on a 180 mm bed.
  • Fit. The puck has 0.3 mm of room on each side in the bay, and the lid's lip has 0.15 mm in the shell. Printers differ. If a part is tight or loose, change bay.clearance or lid.lipClearance and make the files again.
  • These are starting points. No slicer has been run on the files, so print time and the weight of plastic are not known. Printed solid, the tile would be 66 cm³ of plastic; with infill it will be less.

The tap tag

The recess under the tile is 26.6 mm across and 0.6 mm deep, made for a round 25 mm NFC sticker.

  • Stickers. Round NFC stickers are on sale at 22 mm and 25 mm across. One 25 mm sticker is listed as 0.28 mm thick with a 22 mm antenna, and one 22 mm sticker as about 0.14 mm thick. A 30 mm one is listed too, with a note that it has been discontinued. For another size, change tagRecess.stickerDiameter.
  • Chip. All three carry NXP's NTAG213. NXP sells the family as NTAG 213, 215 and 216, with 144, 504 or 888 bytes that a user can write, and says the chips follow the NFC Forum Type 2 Tag and ISO/IEC 14443 Type A specifications.
  • Range. NXP gives an operating distance of up to 100 mm, depending on the reader's field and the antenna. That is not a promise about a phone and a small sticker. Here the tag sits under 7.4 mm of printed plastic. Whether a phone reads it from above is not tested. From below there is nothing in the way.
  • Metal. Both sticker sellers say these tags do not work on metal. On a metal shelf this tag would sit about 1.5 mm above the metal and may not read. Not tested.
  • Heat. The 25 mm sticker is rated from −25 °C to 70 °C. One more reason this place stays away from flame.
  • What goes on the tag is not decided. The open spec says a tag lets a piece and a place tell which is which, and stops there.

Optional: a puck that senses Untested

The open spec says the puck is the part that senses, and that the first one senses heat and light. This is a sketch of one way to try that with parts on sale today: a shell and a vented lid that fit the same bay, four parts to go inside, and how they might be wired.

None of it has been built, and there is no firmware, so nothing here sends a signal yet. A place does not need it. The tile, a blank puck and a tag are complete with nothing plugged in.

    Seeed Studio XIAO nRF52840

    Seeed gives its size as 21 × 17.8 mm and its standby draw as 5 μA. It carries a charging chip for a rechargeable lithium battery.

    Use the plain board. Seeed's “Sense” version of it adds a microphone and a motion sensor, and the open spec allows no microphones.

Wiring sketch

Known doubts about this sketch

  • Power. Seeed describes two ways to power the board: 5 V through its USB-C socket, or a 3.7 V lithium battery on its battery pads, which the board then charges. Its wiki calls the 3V3 pin a power output. This sketch feeds a coin cell into that pin instead. Seeed's pages do not describe doing so, and it has not been tried.
  • Keep the cell away from the charger. A CR2032 is not a rechargeable cell. Do not wire it to the board's battery pads, and switch it off before plugging in USB.
  • Voltage. Adafruit describes a CR2032 as 3 V nominal with about 220 mAh. Its humidity board asks for 3.3 to 5 V and its light board carries a 3.3 V regulator, so on a coin cell both sit below the supply they are described for. They may work. Nobody has checked, and nobody has measured how long a cell would last.
  • Room. The shell's cavity is 42 mm across and 21.6 mm deep. The width was chosen so the 29 × 28 mm cell board lies flat with 0.84 mm to spare at each corner. The depth is a guess: the sellers' pages do not give the height of any of the four parts.
  • Sensing through slots. The lid has 8 slots for air and light. How well a sensor behind them reads a room is unknown.
  • Reaching the switch. The switch ends up inside the shell, under the lid. A physical off should be something a person can see and reach, so a later shell needs an opening for it.
  • The radio is not settled. Bluetooth LE is here because small boards that have it are easy to buy. The open mesh notes still list the radio as an open question, and the spec's rule stands: every networked object has a physical off and is off by default.

How the shapes were checked

The script that writes the files checks each part as it builds it: every edge is shared by exactly two triangles, no triangle is empty, and the surface encloses a volume. A second script, in Python and sharing no code with the first, then reads the finished files. On Nov 11, 2026 it reported, for all four parts:

  • each mesh is watertight, wound one way and a single body;
  • each bounding box matches params.json;
  • each volume matches a calculation done by hand from params.json, to better than one part in ten thousand;
  • of 40,000 random points per part, every one well inside the described shape is inside the mesh and every one well outside is outside, so the bay, the ring, the recess and the feet are where the tables put them;
  • sitting the way it prints, no face overhangs by more than 45°;
  • going straight in from every triangle, the shortest path through plastic is 2.0 mm.

None of that says the parts print well, fit each other or last. Only a printer can say that.

What nobody knows yet

  • Whether it prints cleanly, on any printer.
  • Whether the puck drops in and lifts out, and whether the lid pushes on, at the gaps given.
  • Whether the tile sits flat on its four feet.
  • Whether a phone reads the tag through the tile, and what a phone does when a tagged piece sits on the ring right over the tile's own tag.
  • Everything about the sensing puck.
  • Whether 100 mm is the right size for a place, 46 mm the right puck, or a bay open at the top the right way for a puck to seat. The open spec lists all three as undecided.
