# Thirty-Seven Flowers, Forty Guests

---

Crumb counted the rolls twice.

The second count did nothing to improve the supply.

“Twenty-four,” said the little brass baker.

Hem, who had arrived carrying the saucepan Crumb had borrowed the night before, peered into the basket.

“Large ones.”

“That is their only argument.”

Morning filled the courtyard. Pink petals floated in the fountain. Beyond the tiled roof, the palms stood in a little bank of sea fog, looking as though somebody had rubbed out their feet.

The apprentice was setting flower cards beside the plates.

Thirty-seven cards. Thirty-seven places.

He kept the orange poppy for the chair nearest the gate.

Barley watched from the shady verge, where Bridle had put out his hay. He seemed satisfied with the arrangements, although he briefly inspected Hem’s empty saucepan.

“No screws in this one,” Hem told him.

---

The neighbors arrived with cups, folding stools, a bowl of strawberries, a jar of honey. One brought a table and apologized for its short leg.

Bridle slipped a flat stone beneath it.

“That’s been bothering me for six years,” its owner said.

Then three strangers appeared at the gate.

A woman carried a jar of orange marmalade. A child held an empty flowerpot against his chest. A narrow brass traveler had a violin case strapped to his back.

They had met on the path while trying to locate the bread.

“Is this a private breakfast?” the woman asked.

Crumb looked at the rolls.

Hem looked at the woman’s jar.

“Come in,” they said together.

---

Bridle brought the potting bench. The three visitors sat beneath the jacaranda, their knees arranged around a watering can.

The apprentice stood beside them holding his empty card box.

“I’ll make three more.”

“After you’ve eaten,” said Crumb.

“But everyone else has a flower.”

The child looked inside his pot.

“I was hoping for one too.”

That required a different sort of flower. The apprentice considered it.

Crumb cut the rolls in half. Twenty-four became forty-eight, which was a considerable improvement. From the borrowed saucepan came porridge with toasted oats on top.

The marmalade jar traveled down the table.

By the time it returned, the woman knew the names of eleven neighbors and had promised to lend someone a ladder.

---

After breakfast, the apprentice brought down the little hand press.

He also brought three blank cards, a tin of orange ink, and his biscuit tin to stand on.

“What flowers would you like?”

The brass traveler opened his violin case.

There was a violin inside, to Hem’s obvious relief.

“Something like this.”

He played four notes. The first two stepped upward. The third stayed where it was. The fourth dropped softly into the garden.

The apprentice tried to draw them.

“More room between these,” said the traveler.

They tried again.

Soon there was a flower with four uneven petals, and a small tune that belonged to it.

The woman requested an orange blossom. She helped ink the plate and left a thumbprint beside the stem.

“Oh,” she said.

The apprentice added two tiny leaves to the thumbprint.

Now it was a second blossom, rather low on the branch.

The woman laughed and asked to keep it exactly like that.

---

For the child, Bridle found a small marigold growing in a crowded patch by the wall.

They settled it into the empty pot with fresh soil.

The apprentice printed a marigold card to go with it. While the ink dried, the child wrote on the back in large, careful letters:

**THANK YOU FOR MY FLOWER.**

Then he stopped.

“What’s your name?”

“The apprentice.”

The child waited with his pencil.

The apprentice looked toward Hem, who was listening to the four-note tune. He looked at Crumb, collecting plates. At Bridle, brushing soil from small brass fingers.

“I haven’t chosen one yet.”

“Shall I leave a space?”

“Please.”

The child drew a line beneath the message.

---

Forty guests lingered until the fog lifted.

There were eight half-rolls left, although not for very long.

The apprentice put the thank-you card beside the press. The blank line was wide enough for quite a good name.

Outside, the brass traveler played the flower tune again.

Barley turned one ear toward it.

Then he went back to his hay.
