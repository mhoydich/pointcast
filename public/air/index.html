01 / FIELD GUIDE

The invisible landscape above El Segundo

Air is the part of El Segundo you move through without seeing. It carries marine clouds over the coast, turns a flag, supplies oxygen to a runner and enters an engine. It can also carry pollutants that a blue sky does not reveal. A useful air page begins by separating two questions: What is the atmosphere doing? And what is in it?

Weather observations help with the first. Air-quality measurements help with the second. They meet in the real world, but they are not interchangeable. A west wind is not an AQI reading. High humidity is not a particle concentration. This guide uses a fixed public El Segundo reference point and named regional sources, so you can learn from the surroundings without sharing your own location.

02 / FIELD GUIDE

A global mixture, a local experience

Dry air is approximately 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and 0.93 percent argon, with other gases making up the remainder. “Dry” matters: water vapor varies, changing the proportions of the whole mixture. Trace gases can be important even when their share is small. Amount alone does not tell you a substance's role in climate or health. NOAA: the atmosphere

The atmosphere is a connected system. A thermometer samples conditions at a particular place; it does not describe an entire city. A pollution monitor answers a question about specified pollutants and time intervals. The first skill in reading an environmental dashboard is learning to ask what was actually measured before interpreting the number.

03 / FIELD GUIDE

The ocean changes the air overhead

Southern California's marine layer is a familiar lesson in vertical structure. Cool marine air can sit below warmer air aloft, with an inversion limiting vertical mixing. Low clouds form when conditions permit; the layer and the clouds are related but not synonyms. Sea-breeze circulation can help move marine air onshore. Terrain influences how far the low cloud layer reaches inland. NWS: marine-layer processes

That makes a coastal morning a good observational puzzle. Where does the cloud edge sit? Does the wind change before or after the clouds break? Write down what you see, then compare it with a timestamped station record. A pattern worth investigating is not yet a demonstrated cause. A few mornings cannot establish a climate trend.

04 / FIELD GUIDE

What the nearby weather station knows

The Los Angeles International Airport observing station, KLAX, is a useful nearby weather source. NWS lists it at 33.93806° N, 118.38889° W, about 3.3 kilometers from this guide's reference point. It measures airport conditions, not every block of El Segundo. Buildings, exposure and distance from the shore can matter. NWS KLAX conditions and coordinates

Temperature, humidity, pressure and wind belong together, but each retains its own meaning. Wind direction names where the wind comes from: a west wind travels from west toward east. Dew point is the temperature to which air would need to cool to reach saturation under the usual meteorological definition. Do not read an airport's wind arrow as a map of pollutant concentration. NOAA: interpreting surface observations

05 / FIELD GUIDE

The pollutants have different stories

PM2.5 refers to fine particles with aerodynamic diameters generally no greater than 2.5 micrometers. Particles may be emitted directly or form through atmospheric chemistry. Their small size helps explain why they can reach deep into the respiratory system. A reported concentration describes mass per volume of air, commonly micrograms per cubic meter. It is not itself an AQI score. EPA: particle pollution

Ground-level ozone is different: it forms through reactions involving nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight. It can also travel with the wind. The same oxygen-based molecule serves a protective role high in the stratosphere and is harmful to breathe near the ground. Location and chemistry matter as much as the name. EPA: ground-level ozone

06 / FIELD GUIDE

How to read AQI without overreading it

The U.S. Air Quality Index translates pollutant information into a health-communication scale. Its six categories run from good to hazardous. A value of 100 is a reference on that scale, not “100 percent polluted.” Always keep the pollutant, category, reporting area and timestamp with the number. AirNow: AQI basics

A current AQI is also not an instantaneous chemical reading. AirNow uses NowCast methods to relate recent measurements to an index for current conditions. Daily indices and forecasts have different time meanings. The reporting-area observation can reflect the highest AQI among reporting monitors, rather than a sensor located in the ZIP code you entered. AirNow: using the index · AirNow: reporting areas

07 / FIELD GUIDE

Nearby does not mean interchangeable

Monitor names deserve the same scrutiny as numbers. South Coast AQMD's July 2026 network plan lists LAX Hastings as offline during relocation. An old station profile is not evidence of a current feed. The active West Los Angeles site measures ozone and nitrogen dioxide; its published 2026 profile does not list PM2.5. Compton has a continuous PM2.5 instrument, but it is about 19.6 kilometers from the guide's reference point. Neither should silently become an “El Segundo sensor.” AQMD 2026 network plan

For current guidance, use AirNow and South Coast AQMD's regional information, preserving the geography they actually report. For questions about specific facilities and pollutants, the district also maintains refinery community and fenceline monitoring resources. Those answer different questions from a citywide AQI card; a reading alone does not establish which source caused it. AQMD refinery monitoring

08 / FIELD GUIDE

A history of learning what was in the sky

Los Angeles' air-pollution history is a history of investigation. South Coast AQMD records a major smog event on July 26, 1943, followed by new public institutions and scientific work on photochemical pollution. Understanding the problem required looking beyond visible smoke to reactions among gases and sunlight. That lesson still applies to a beautifully clear day: visibility is one observation, not a complete chemical analysis. AQMD: historical perspective

Air is also an economic resource. EPA's assessments connect cleaner air with fewer illnesses, fewer missed school and work days, and reduced damage to crops. Such benefits are often less visible than the equipment used to control emissions. The useful economic question includes who bears exposure and who benefits from improvement, alongside what a control measure costs. EPA: clean air and the economy

09 / FIELD GUIDE

An ecosystem includes its atmosphere

Plants exchange gases with the air. Elevated ozone can damage sensitive vegetation and affect ecosystems; air quality is therefore a living-landscape issue as well as a human-health issue. Coastal habitat restoration and air-pollution reduction operate on different parts of a connected system. EPA: air, animals and plants

A small personal sensor can make that connection tangible, but precision in the display is not proof of accuracy. EPA recommends evaluating sensors against reference instruments through side-by-side comparison, called collocation. Treat a low-cost sensor as an instrument with limitations, not an automatic verdict about a neighborhood or a source. EPA: sensor collocation

10 / FIELD GUIDE

A practical way to look up

Read the current official AQI and its activity guidance before deciding how to use outdoor air, especially if you are sensitive to pollution. Ozone can irritate airways, and people with asthma, children, older adults and those active outdoors can be at greater risk. Follow relevant public-health advice and your own clinician's guidance. This page teaches interpretation; it does not diagnose symptoms or promise that a particular route is safe. EPA: ozone and health

The enduring habit is simple: notice, measure, name the uncertainty, then compare. A good question about today's sky can become tomorrow's small experiment.

11 / FIELD GUIDE

Supplement: pocket glossary

  • AQI: a unitless U.S. index communicating air-quality-related health concern; not a pollutant concentration.
  • PM2.5: fine-particle pollution defined by an aerodynamic size cut, generally 2.5 micrometers or smaller.
  • Ozone: O3; protective high in the atmosphere, harmful when breathed at ground level.
  • Concentration: amount of a pollutant in a specified amount of air, with units and averaging time.
  • NowCast: an EPA method relating recent pollutant readings to current AQI reporting.
  • Relative humidity: moisture relative to saturation at the current temperature, expressed as a percentage.
  • Dew point: a temperature-based measure related to how close air is to saturation.
  • Inversion: a layer in which temperature increases with height, limiting vertical mixing under suitable conditions.
  • Wind direction: the direction the wind comes from, conventionally measured clockwise from north.

13 / FIELD GUIDE

University of El Segundo: three short exercises

1. Classify the card · 10 minutes

Sort “west wind,” “PM2.5 12 μg/m³,” “AQI 42,” and “tomorrow's forecast” into weather, concentration, index and prediction. These are teaching examples, not current readings. Success: four labels plus the units or time information each item still needs.

2. Follow the wind arrow · 15 minutes

At a safe outdoor spot, sketch which direction a flag or tree moves. Compare with KLAX's wind-from direction and observation time. Success: a diagram that distinguishes “from” and “toward,” plus two reasons the airport and your sheltered spot may differ. Do not identify a pollution source from this sketch.

3. Read the timestamp · 10 minutes

Find the time attached to an official air-quality report. Separate observation time, forecast date and the time you opened the page. Success: a one-sentence explanation of why refreshing a page does not make an old measurement new.

14 / FIELD GUIDE

University of El Segundo: three practical projects

A marine-layer diary · seven mornings, free

From the same safe public viewing spot or a window, record cloud cover, apparent cloud edge, time and a brief sketch. Add KLAX temperature, humidity and wind only when available and timestamped. Deliverable: a one-page sequence with one proposed explanation and one alternative. No weather record is required to manufacture a missing cloud observation.

An air-source atlas · one afternoon

Create a small map of KLAX, West Los Angeles and Compton monitors using the published coordinates. Add a table of pollutant coverage and source links. Put the El Segundo public reference point on the map, not a home address. Deliverable: a guide answering “Which source can answer which question?” Include LAX Hastings in an offline-history note, not the active layer.

An honest AQI reader · beginner data design

Prototype the interface before connecting data: category name, pollutant, actual reporting area, observation time, source and status. Test fixtures for missing data, an old timestamp and a forecast-only result. Keep all fixtures labeled “example.” Deliverable: a working state diagram or small component that can say “AQI unavailable here; check the official source” without replacing AQI with weather.
