The commons recipe

Six ingredients, in order. It works for a house, a shed, a kitchen or a boat. Begin with people and a weekend, and leave property for much later or never.

Plainly

  • This is a study and a public resource. It is not an offer.
  • Nothing is being bought or sold. No money is being raised. There are no listings here.
  • It is not legal, tax or investment advice.
  • Tradable shares in property are likely securities. Anyone going near that needs a securities lawyer first.
  • Rules change. Each rule here was read on November 11, 2026; open the linked page and call the planning counter before acting on any of it.

Six ingredients

  1. The first ten people

    Ten is enough to share a cost and small enough to fit round one table. One seat, one voice. Agree how a seat is given up before anyone takes one.

  2. A rented weekend first

    Before any lease, try two nights together somewhere it is lawful: a permitted rental, a campground, a hostel, a member's hosted home. The town pages show where a whole-house weekend is and is not allowed. You are testing the people, not the building.

  3. House rules

    One page, written by the ten, signed by the ten. Quiet hours, guests, cleaning, keys, money, how to disagree, how to leave.

  4. Open books

    One ledger anyone in the group can read at any time, and that a neighbor can ask to see. Every amount has a date, a name and a reason.

  5. Use-rights, not ownership

    A member holds a turn at the place: so many days, so many keys. A turn is not a share of the property and cannot be sold. The aim is a club, not an investment. Whether a particular arrangement really is one is a lawyer's question, and anything a member could sell or profit from needs a securities lawyer before it exists.

  6. A public-good share

    Set aside a fixed part of the calendar and the budget for people who are not members: free youth surf mornings, an open bench day, a supper anyone can join. Decide the fraction at the start and print it on the door.

HOUSE RULES (one page)
1. Who we are: ten seats, one voice each.
2. What we share, and the days each member may use it.
3. Quiet hours and neighbors.
4. Guests: how many, how often, who answers for them.
5. Cleaning, repairs and what to do when something breaks.
6. Keys and safety.
7. Money: what each pays, when, and where the ledger lives.
8. The public-good share: how much, and what it is spent on.
9. Disagreements: who decides, and how.
10. Leaving: notice, and what happens to the seat. A seat is never sold.

It does not have to be a surf house

  • Surf house. Boards, wetsuits, a rinse, a bunk or two where the town allows it.
  • Workshop. Benches and the tools that are too big or too rarely used to own alone.
  • Community kitchen. A real stove, a long table, a shared pantry, a supper night.
  • Music room. Instruments on the wall, soft walls, posted quiet hours.
  • Garden. Beds, compost, a tool shed, a harvest table.
  • Board library. Borrow a board like a book. A card, a due date, a repair jar.
  • Boat. A small sailboat, a skiff or a rack of kayaks with a booking sheet.

Before anyone signs anything

  • Ask the city whether the stay or the use is allowed at that address. Each town page links the official pages and lists five questions to take to the planning counter.
  • Near the water, ask who issues the coastal permit. The Coastal Commission says development in the coastal zone generally needs a permit first, from the local government where there is a certified coastal program and from the Commission where there is not. California Coastal Commission: permit applications (read 2026-11-11)
  • If the plan ever includes ownership, pooled money held for people, or anything a member could sell, stop and hire a securities lawyer and a tax adviser. This page cannot tell you what is lawful for your group. No securities regulator page was opened for this study; that is not verified.
