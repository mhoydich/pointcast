South Bay Field Guide

A jet-age field guide to the world of Signal Over the South Bay: 100 nature wonders across eight habitats, 40 animals and pets, and a map of how it all connects. Every entry is tagged REAL or INVENTED.

REAL A real South Bay species, place or phenomenon. Descriptions and field notes are written to be accurate; check a local naturalist guide before relying on them.

INVENTED Part of the comic's fictional world. Invented plants, animals, robo-pets and links are story, not science. Their notes are marked "From the comic".

100 nature entries (72 real, 28 invented) and 40 animals (19 real wildlife, 21 invented pets, robots and orbit creatures).

Dockweiler Dunes & Beach

13 entries · 11 real · 2 invented

REALN001

Sea Fig

Carpobrotus chilensis

A trailing succulent of the ice plant family that mats over sand with fat, three-sided leaves. Its magenta flowers open in full sun and close when the marine layer rolls in.

Field note: Each leaf is a little canteen: the plant stores water inside so it can live on sand that drains in seconds.

See also: Wrack Line, Jet-Wash Dune Grass

REALN002

Beach Evening Primrose

Camissoniopsis cheiranthifolia

A low, silver-gray dune plant that spreads in a rosette across the sand. Its four-petaled yellow flowers brighten the foredunes from spring into fall.

Field note: The yellow blossoms blush reddish as they age, so one plant can wear two colors at once.

See also: Beach Evening Primrose, Beach Sand Verbena

REALN003

Beach Sand Verbena

Abronia umbellata

A sprawling native with round clusters of pink-to-magenta flowers on long sandy runners. It is one of the first colors you see on a restored dune.

Field note: Its leaves are sticky, so they wear a coat of sand grains, a kind of beach camouflage and sunscreen.

See also: Beach Evening Primrose, Silver Beach Lupine

REALN004

Silver Beach Lupine

Lupinus chamissonis

A rounded shrub of soft silver leaves and spikes of lavender-blue pea flowers. It anchors the back dunes and feeds the soil around it.

Field note: Fine silver hairs on its leaves bounce sunlight away, keeping the plant cool on a blazing dune.

See also: Deerweed, El Segundo Blue Butterfly

REALN005

Beach Bur

Ambrosia chamissonis

A gray-green, sprawling dune plant with deeply cut leaves. Its spiny little burs hitch rides on fur, feathers and beach towels.

Field note: It is a cousin of ragweed and one of the toughest pioneer plants on a moving dune.

See also: Beach Bur, Sea Fig

REALN006

Grunion Run

Leuresthes tenuis

On spring and summer nights just after the highest tides, silver grunion surf onto the beach to spawn in the wet sand. The eggs incubate in the sand until the next big tides wash them out to hatch.

Field note: Grunion are among the only fish in the world that leave the water entirely to lay their eggs.

See also: Mudflat, The Moon, Brown Pelican

REALN007

Pacific Mole Crab

Emerita analoga

The little sand crab that kids dig up in the swash zone, shaped like an egg with legs. It rides each wave up the beach and burrows backward in a flash.

Field note: It eats by holding up feathery antennae in the backwash to comb plankton from the water.

See also: Western Snowy Plover, Western Gull

REALN008

Beach Hopper

Megalorchestia californiana

A pale, long-antennaed amphipod that hides under kelp by day and boings across the sand at night. It is the beach's cleanup crew.

Field note: Beach hoppers navigate by the moon and the slope of the beach to find their way back to the wrack line.

See also: Beach Hopper, Western Snowy Plover

REALN009

Wrack Line

The ribbon of washed-up kelp and seagrass that marks the last high tide. It is a buffet for flies and hoppers, and those feed the shorebirds.

Field note: Beach groomers that rake the wrack away also rake away the food that snowy plover chicks depend on.

See also: Giant Kelp, Pacific Mole Crab, Western Snowy Plover

REALN010

Sea Glass

Bottle glass that has been tumbled by surf and sand until it turns frosted and smooth. Locals keep jars of it on windowsills all along the coast.

Field note: It can take 20 years or more of tumbling to frost a shard, and red and orange pieces are among the rarest finds.

See also: Wrack Line

REALN011

Marine Layer

A low gray blanket of cloud that drifts in from the cold Pacific on spring and summer mornings. It usually burns off by midday and creeps back at dusk.

Field note: Locals call its seasons May Gray and June Gloom; it keeps the beach towns cooler than inland LA.

See also: Fog Harp, Contrail Clouds, Santa Ana Winds

INVENTEDN012

Signal Sand

In this world, a strip of quartz sand near the pier that faintly hums whenever the Lantern broadcasts. Beachgoers press an ear to the sand to hear the morning edition early.

From the comic (fiction): Invented for the comic: the sand is said to go silent at 4:12 A.M., which is how the town knew something was wrong.

See also: Moonbounce, Signal Sand

INVENTEDN013

Jet-Wash Dune Grass

An invented dune grass whose seed heads lean toward the runways and fly off in the wake of every takeoff. It spreads along the coast one departure at a time.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: field notes claim you can tell the last flight's direction by the way the grass is combed.

See also: Marine Layer, Contrail Clouds

Kelp Forest & Tide Pools

13 entries · 11 real · 2 invented

REALN014

Giant Kelp

Macrocystis pyrifera

A golden-brown seaweed that rises from the rocky bottom to the surface in towering underwater forests. Gas-filled bulbs hold its blades up toward the light.

Field note: In good conditions it can grow up to about two feet in a single day.

See also: Purple Sea Urchin, Garibaldi, Solar Kelp

REALN015

Feather Boa Kelp

Egregia menziesii

A long, strap-shaped brown kelp fringed with tiny blades that make it look like a feather boa. It drapes over rocks in the low intertidal.

Field note: Its frond can stretch longer than a city bus and still flex with every wave.

See also: Giant Kelp, Surfgrass

REALN016

Eelgrass

Zostera marina

A bright green, ribbon-leaved plant that forms underwater meadows in calm bays and harbors. Its beds shelter baby fish, crabs and scallops.

Field note: Eelgrass is not seaweed; it is a true flowering plant that blooms and pollinates underwater.

See also: Surfgrass, Leopard Shark

REALN017

Surfgrass

Phyllospadix torreyi

A vivid emerald flowering plant that grows in shaggy mats on surf-swept rocks. At low tide it lies combed flat like wet hair.

Field note: Like eelgrass it is a true seagrass, holding on with roots in places that would rip most plants away.

See also: Eelgrass, Giant Green Anemone

REALN018

Giant Green Anemone

Anthopleura xanthogrammica

A big, jade-green sea anemone that lines tide pools with a ring of sticky tentacles. It catches mussels, crabs and anything the waves knock loose.

Field note: Its green glow comes partly from tiny algae living inside its tissues and sharing the sunlight.

See also: California Mussel Bed, Ochre Sea Star

REALN019

Ochre Sea Star

Pisaster ochraceus

A sturdy, five-armed sea star in purple or orange that clings to rocks in the mid-tide zone. It pries open mussels with steady pull.

Field note: It is the original keystone species: scientists found that removing it lets mussels take over the whole shore.

See also: California Mussel Bed, Giant Green Anemone

REALN020

Purple Sea Urchin

Strongylocentrotus purpuratus

A spiny violet grazer that wedges itself into rock and munches drift kelp. When predators disappear, urchins can swarm and mow down a forest.

Field note: Bare rock left behind by too many urchins is called an urchin barren.

See also: Giant Kelp, California Sea Lion

REALN021

California Mussel Bed

Mytilus californianus

Dense blue-black beds of mussels that coat the rocks where waves crash hardest. Hundreds of small creatures live in the spaces between shells.

Field note: Mussels anchor with silky byssal threads strong enough that engineers study them for underwater glues.

See also: Ochre Sea Star, Giant Green Anemone

REALN022

Owl Limpet

Lottia gigantea

A large, flat limpet that tends a private patch of algae on the rocks. It bulldozes intruders off its turf.

Field note: Owl limpets start life male and can turn female as they grow larger.

See also: California Mussel Bed

REALN023

Blue-Glow Tide

Lingulodinium polyedra

A bloom of plankton that tints the water reddish by day and makes breaking waves flash electric blue at night. It shows up some years along Southern California beaches.

Field note: The glow is bioluminescence: each tiny cell flashes when the water jostles it.

See also: Mudlight Algae, Beacon Moss

REALN024

California Two-Spot Octopus

Octopus bimaculoides

A small, shy octopus that hides in rocky crevices and tide pools. A blue eyespot ring sits below each eye.

Field note: It can change both color and skin texture in a fraction of a second to vanish against a rock.

See also: Purple Sea Urchin, California Mussel Bed

INVENTEDN025

Tide-Clock Barnacles

An invented acorn barnacle colony on the pier pilings that opens all at once at 6:45 every morning. Pier fishermen set their watches by them.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: they stayed shut on the morning the Lantern went dark, and opened late, right when the signal returned.

See also: Moonbounce, Tidewatch Lily

INVENTEDN026

Radio Abalone

An invented abalone whose rainbow shell lining catches stray broadcasts like a crystal radio. Divers hear faint music when they hold one to their ear.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: real abalone are protected in California, so in this world the radio kind are only listened to, never taken.

See also: Giant Kelp, Moonbounce

El Segundo Blue Preserve

12 entries · 10 real · 2 invented

REALN027

El Segundo Blue Butterfly

Euphilotes allyni

A thumbnail-size butterfly found only in a few coastal dune remnants around El Segundo and the LAX dunes. Males are bright blue above; females are brown with an orange band.

Field note: It was among the first insects listed under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1976, and it lives almost its whole life on one plant.

See also: Seacliff Buckwheat, El Segundo Blue Caterpillar, LAX Dunes

REALN028

Seacliff Buckwheat

Eriogonum parvifolium

A low native shrub with small leaves and flat clusters of cream-to-pink flowers. It is the El Segundo Blue's only host plant.

Field note: Restoration crews plant it by the thousands; more buckwheat means more butterflies the next summer.

See also: El Segundo Blue Butterfly, El Segundo Blue Caterpillar

REALN029

El Segundo Blue Caterpillar

Euphilotes allyni

The tiny larva feeds on buckwheat flowers in summer and then drops into the sand below to pupate. It waits there, sometimes for more than a year, until the next bloom.

Field note: Its pink-and-cream coloring matches the buckwheat flower heads it eats.

See also: El Segundo Blue Butterfly, Seacliff Buckwheat

REALN030

LAX Dunes

The largest surviving stretch of the old El Segundo dune system, beside the airport's runways. Fenced and restored, it is home base for the butterfly.

Field note: Once, these dunes stretched for miles from Westchester to Palos Verdes; the preserve is a small saved piece.

See also: El Segundo Blue Butterfly, Jet-Wash Dune Grass

REALN031

Coastal Sagebrush

Artemisia californica

A soft, gray-green native shrub with threadlike leaves and a sharp herbal scent. It is a backbone plant of coastal sage scrub.

Field note: Old-timers called it cowboy cologne for the smell it leaves on your hands.

See also: Deerweed, Coastal Sagebrush

REALN032

Deerweed

Acmispon glaber

A wiry, green-stemmed native shrub dotted with small yellow pea flowers that age to red. It is often first to grow back after a disturbance.

Field note: Its roots host bacteria that pull nitrogen from the air, fertilizing the dune for everything else.

See also: Silver Beach Lupine, Coastal Sagebrush

REALN033

California Poppy

Eschscholzia californica

The state flower, with silky orange cups that light up open ground in spring. It pops up along the dune edges and roadsides.

Field note: Poppies fold their petals shut at night and on cloudy days, and open again in the sun.

See also: California Poppy, Coral Tree

REALN034

Globose Dune Beetle

Coelus globosus

A small, round, dark beetle that lives in loose coastal sand. It is rare and depends on healthy native dunes.

Field note: It cannot fly; it swims through the sand just under the surface instead.

See also: LAX Dunes

REALN035

Mock Heather

Ericameria ericoides

A dense, needle-leaved native shrub that blooms in bursts of tiny yellow flowers in late summer and fall. Bees crowd it when little else is flowering.

Field note: Its late bloom is a lifeline for pollinators at the end of the dry season.

See also: Deerweed, Seacliff Buckwheat

REALN036

California Croton

Croton californicus

A small, silvery dune plant with felted leaves and tiny greenish flowers. It holds loose sand in place.

Field note: Each plant is either male or female, so it takes two to make seed.

See also: LAX Dunes

INVENTEDN037

Blue-Hour Buckwheat

An invented buckwheat that opens only at dusk, when the Lantern's beacon first glows. The butterflies in this world take a second, evening flight to visit it.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: the flowers are said to glow faintly teal, matching the beacon above.

See also: Seacliff Buckwheat, Moonbounce

INVENTEDN038

Periwinkle Static

An invented shimmer of static charge that sparkles off El Segundo Blue wings on dry Santa Ana afternoons. It looks like blue glitter in the air above the preserve.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: on the field guide's map it is the reason the preserve fence hums.

See also: El Segundo Blue Butterfly, Santa Ana Winds

Ballona Wetlands

13 entries · 11 real · 2 invented

REALN039

Pickleweed

Salicornia pacifica

A succulent marsh plant made of jointed green stems that look like strings of tiny pickles. It carpets the salt marsh in green that turns red in fall.

Field note: It soaks up salt into its stem tips, which turn red and drop off, taking the salt with them.

See also: Belding's Savannah Sparrow, Pacific Cordgrass

REALN040

Pacific Cordgrass

Spartina foliosa

A tall marsh grass that grows along the lowest, wettest edge of the salt marsh. Tides flood it twice a day.

Field note: It pushes extra salt out through its leaves, leaving glittering crystals on the blades.

See also: Pickleweed, Great Blue Heron

REALN041

Saltgrass

Distichlis spicata

A low, tough grass that forms mats on the higher, drier parts of the marsh. It spreads by underground runners.

Field note: It literally sweats salt: tiny crystals sparkle on its leaves on sunny days.

See also: Pickleweed

REALN042

Alkali Heath

Frankenia salina

A low, gray-green marsh shrub with tiny pink flowers. It edges salt pans where little else will grow.

Field note: It handles salty, waterlogged soil that would kill most garden plants.

See also: Alkali Heath

REALN043

Arroyo Willow

Salix lasiolepis

A shrubby willow that lines the freshwater marsh and creek edges. Songbirds nest in its thickets.

Field note: Willow bark contains salicin, the natural compound behind aspirin.

See also: Allen's Hummingbird, Tidal Creek

REALN044

Striped Shore Crab

Pachygrapsus crassipes

A square-bodied crab with green and purple stripes that scuttles over rocks and marsh banks. It eats algae and whatever it can scavenge.

Field note: It can stay out of water for long stretches and even climbs walls and pilings.

See also: Striped Shore Crab, Great Blue Heron

REALN045

California Horn Snail

Cerithideopsis californica

A small, tall-spired brown snail that crowds the mudflats by the thousands. It grazes the film of algae on the mud.

Field note: At low tide whole mudflats can be dotted with them like scattered rice.

See also: King Tide

REALN046

Tidal Creek

A winding channel that fills and drains the marsh with every tide. Fish, crabs and birds follow the water in and out.

Field note: The creek's curves are made by the tide itself, carving new bends year after year.

See also: Mudflat, King Tide, Tidal Creek

REALN047

Mudflat

Shining mud that appears when the tide drops, packed with worms, clams and snails. It is a dinner table for shorebirds.

Field note: A single square yard of good mudflat can hold thousands of small animals.

See also: Striped Shore Crab, Great Blue Heron

REALN048

King Tide

The highest tides of the year, when the sun and moon line up close to Earth in winter. Water reaches far up beaches and into the marsh.

Field note: Scientists photograph king tides to preview where everyday sea levels may reach in the future.

See also: The Moon, Grunion Run, Pacific Cordgrass

REALN049

Ballona Creek

A long, mostly concrete-lined channel that drains much of LA's westside into the bay at Playa del Rey. Near the mouth, the tide still pushes in.

Field note: On a rising tide, salt water flows backward up the creek past the bike path.

See also: Pacific Cordgrass, Great Blue Heron

INVENTEDN050

Tide-Tuned Reeds

An invented reed that whistles the PointCast station chime when the evening breeze crosses the marsh. Birders say it is how the herons know it is time to fish.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: each hollow stalk is said to be cut to a different note by the tide.

See also: Pacific Cordgrass, Moonbounce

INVENTEDN051

Mudlight Algae

An invented glowing algae that traces the tide line across the mudflats at night. From the air it draws the shape of the marsh in teal light.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: the Gull's pilots use it as a landing light when the marine layer is thick.

See also: King Tide, Blue-Glow Tide

Palos Verdes Bluffs

12 entries · 10 real · 2 invented

REALN052

Santa Ana Winds

Hot, dry winds that pour down from the deserts toward the coast, mostly in fall and winter. They bring crystal-clear views and high fire danger.

Field note: Offshore Santa Anas hold up the face of each wave and blow spray backward off the crest, a surfer's favorite sight.

See also: Marine Layer, Catalina on the Horizon

REALN053

Coastal Prickly Pear

Opuntia littoralis

A sprawling cactus with flat green pads and yellow flowers that grows on dry coastal slopes. Its purple fruit feeds birds and coyotes.

Field note: Cactus wrens and other animals use prickly pear thickets as safe hideouts from predators.

See also: Urban Coyote

REALN054

Lemonade Berry

Rhus integrifolia

A tough evergreen shrub with leathery leaves and clusters of sticky, reddish berries. It hugs the bluff tops above the sea.

Field note: Its berries are coated in a tart resin that people have soaked in water for a lemonade-like drink.

See also: Toyon

REALN055

Toyon

Heteromeles arbutifolia

A big native shrub with white summer flowers and bright red winter berries. Birds strip it clean by January.

Field note: It is often called California holly, and Hollywood is popularly said to be named after it.

See also: Lemonade Berry, Allen's Hummingbird

REALN056

Palos Verdes Blue Butterfly

Glaucopsyche lygdamus palosverdesensis

A rare small blue butterfly found only on the Palos Verdes Peninsula. It feeds as a caterpillar on locoweed and deerweed.

Field note: It was thought extinct in the 1980s until a small population was rediscovered in 1994.

See also: El Segundo Blue Butterfly, Deerweed

REALN057

Bush Sunflower

Encelia californica

A rounded shrub covered in yellow daisy flowers with dark centers. It brightens the bluffs after winter rains.

Field note: In summer drought it drops many leaves to save water, then leafs out again with the rains.

See also: Cliff Aster

REALN058

Catalina on the Horizon

On clear days Santa Catalina Island floats on the horizon about 20 miles off the peninsula. After a Santa Ana it looks close enough to swim to.

Field note: Locals read Catalina like a weather station: if you can see it sharply, the air is dry and clean.

See also: Santa Ana Winds, Green Flash

REALN059

Green Flash

A brief green spark at the top of the sun just as it sinks below a clear ocean horizon. The bluffs are one of the best places to try to see it.

Field note: It is caused by the atmosphere bending green light more than red, like a giant prism.

See also: Catalina on the Horizon

REALN060

Portuguese Bend Landslide

A huge slow-moving landslide on the south side of the peninsula. Roads there are patched constantly as the land creeps toward the sea.

Field note: It is one of the most active landslides in the United States; the ground has been moving for decades.

See also: Coastal Prickly Pear

REALN061

Cliff Aster

Malacothrix saxatilis

A wiry native plant with white to pale pink dandelion-like flowers that clings to cliff faces. It blooms over a long season.

Field note: It roots in cracks where you would swear no soil exists.

See also: Bush Sunflower

INVENTEDN062

Updraft Thistle

An invented thistle whose parachute seeds ride the cliff updrafts for miles. Glider pilots follow the seed clouds to find lift.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: Aero Works test pilots call a good updraft day a thistle day.

See also: Santa Ana Winds, Contrail Clouds

INVENTEDN063

Fog-Signal Lichen

An invented orange crust lichen on the bluff rocks that pulses gently whenever the lighthouse sweeps past. Sailors used to say it echoed the beam.

From the comic (fiction): Invented, though real orange lichens do paint California sea cliffs in bright crusts.

See also: Marine Layer, Moonbounce

Main Street Trees & Gardens

13 entries · 10 real · 3 invented

REALN064

Coral Tree

Erythrina caffra

A wide, spreading tree that bursts into red-orange claw-shaped flowers in late winter. Many line boulevards across the Westside.

Field note: It is the official tree of the City of Los Angeles.

See also: Bird of Paradise, Allen's Hummingbird

REALN065

Jacaranda

Jacaranda mimosifolia

A street tree that turns violet-blue with trumpet flowers in late spring. Fallen blossoms carpet sidewalks and parked cars.

Field note: Locals measure late spring by when the jacaranda purple shows up.

See also: Coral Tree

REALN066

Mexican Fan Palm

Washingtonia robusta

The tall, skinny palm with a little round head that defines the Southern California skyline. Many were planted a century ago.

Field note: Some reach over 80 feet tall, wobbling in the wind like giant dandelions.

See also: Peregrine Falcon

REALN067

Bougainvillea

Bougainvillea spectabilis

A thorny vine that pours over walls and garages in magenta, orange and red. It thrives in sun and neglect.

Field note: The bright color comes from papery bracts; the true flowers are the tiny white ones in the middle.

See also: Coral Tree

REALN068

Bird of Paradise

Strelitzia reginae

An orange and blue flower shaped like a crested bird's head, rising on stiff stalks. It grows in nearly every South Bay garden.

Field note: It is the official flower of the City of Los Angeles.

See also: Coral Tree, Allen's Hummingbird

REALN069

Backyard Meyer Lemon

Citrus × meyeri

The lemon tree in the back corner of half the yards in town, heavy with fruit most of the year. Neighbors trade bags of them over the fence.

Field note: Meyer lemons are thought to be a cross between a lemon and a mandarin, which is why they taste sweeter.

See also: Crumb

REALN070

California Sycamore

Platanus racemosa

A big native shade tree with patchy, peeling white-and-tan bark and broad maple-like leaves. It shades parks and old streets.

Field note: Its bark peels off in jigsaw pieces as the trunk grows, so no two trunks look alike.

See also: Red-tailed Hawk, Arroyo Willow

REALN071

Nasturtium

Tropaeolum majus

A sprawling garden plant with round lily-pad leaves and orange and yellow flowers. It spills out of yards and vacant lots all over town.

Field note: Both the flowers and leaves are edible and taste peppery, like watercress.

See also: Newsprint Ivy

REALN072

Moreton Bay Fig

Ficus macrophylla

A giant fig with a broad canopy and huge roots that ripple across the ground. Old ones are local landmarks.

Field note: Its buttress roots can spread wider than the tree is tall.

See also: California Sycamore

REALN073

Front-Yard Aloe

Aloe arborescens

A spiky, branching succulent that blooms with orange-red torches in winter. It needs almost no water.

Field note: Its winter flowers are a key fuel stop for hummingbirds when little else is blooming.

See also: Allen's Hummingbird

INVENTEDN074

Transistor Tulips

An invented garden bulb with cup-shaped petals that swivel toward the strongest broadcast. Front yards bloom in neat rows all facing the Lantern.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: during the blackout, every tulip in town drooped at once.

See also: Moonbounce, Transistor Tulips

INVENTEDN075

Streamline Topiary

An invented hedge that grows on its own into jet-age shapes: fins, swoops and rocket noses. Gardeners only have to keep it watered.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: the Aero Works lobby keeps one trimmed like the Gull.

See also: Newsprint Ivy

INVENTEDN076

Newsprint Ivy

An invented ivy whose cream leaves show faint gray patterns each morning, as if the weather report were printed on them. It climbs porch rails all along Main Street.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: superstitious readers say a blank leaf means a slow news day.

See also: Moonbounce, Nasturtium

Night Sky & Orbit

12 entries · 9 real · 3 invented

REALN077

The Moon

Earth's companion, whose pull raises and lowers the tides twice a day. Over the bay, a rising full moon lays a silver road across the water.

Field note: In Issue 1, the Lantern's knocked-off dish was beaming at the moon instead of El Segundo.

See also: Mudflat, Grunion Run, Signal Aurora

REALN078

Evening Star

Venus, the brightest planet, often shines low in the west right after sunset or in the east before dawn. It is the first light many people see each evening.

Field note: Venus is so bright that it can cast faint shadows on a dark night far from city lights.

See also: The Moon

REALN079

Orion

The great winter constellation, with three bright stars in a row for its belt. It rises over the South Bay on winter evenings.

Field note: Below the belt hangs the Orion Nebula, a star nursery you can see as a fuzzy spot with binoculars.

See also: Milky Way

REALN080

Perseid Meteor Shower

Each August, Earth passes through dust left by Comet Swift-Tuttle and meteors streak across the sky. The best hours are after midnight.

Field note: Perseid meteors hit the upper atmosphere at about 37 miles per second.

See also: Lantern-Belt Micrometeors

INVENTEDN081

Lantern-Belt Micrometeors

An invented band of glittering dust that the Lantern's orbit crosses a few nights a year. A heavy shower of it knocked the dish off its mark in Issue 1.

From the comic (fiction): Invented for the comic, but the station's dish shields were rebuilt after Issue 1.

See also: Perseid Meteor Shower, Moonbounce

REALN082

Rocket Twilight Plume

When a rocket launches from the Central Coast just after sunset or before sunrise, its exhaust plume can glow in sunlight high above the dark ground. People across Southern California see a glowing jellyfish shape.

Field note: The plume shines because it is so high that it is still in sunlight while the ground below is dark.

See also: Contrail Clouds

REALN083

Satellite Pass

A steady point of light gliding silently across the sky a little after dusk or before dawn. Unlike a plane, it does not blink.

Field note: Satellites are visible because they catch sunlight high above while the ground is already in shadow.

See also: Moonbounce

REALN084

Milky Way

Our home galaxy, seen edge-on as a faint river of stars. City light hides it from town, but a dark trip south reveals it.

Field note: Every star you can see without a telescope is part of the Milky Way.

See also: Orion

REALN085

Earthshine

A faint glow on the dark part of a crescent moon. It is sunlight bouncing off Earth, lighting the moon's night side.

Field note: Leonardo da Vinci was the first to explain earthshine correctly, about 500 years ago.

See also: The Moon

REALN086

Moonbounce

Radio operators really do bounce signals off the moon and hear them return to Earth about two and a half seconds later. Ham radio fans call it EME, Earth-Moon-Earth.

Field note: It is a real hobby; in the comic, the Lantern accidentally did it all night.

See also: The Moon, Moonbounce

INVENTEDN087

The Lantern Beacon

The comic's floating broadcast station, seen from town as a steady orange star high over the bay. Its signal carries the morning edition at 6:45.

From the comic (fiction): Invented: this is the heart of the ecosystem map; half the inventions in this guide tune to it.

How the South Bay ecosystem works

Solid lines: real processes. Dashed orange lines: invented, comic-world only.

Start with the moon. Its pull raises and lowers the Pacific twice a day, and those tides run the whole coast. They flood and drain the tide pools, push salt water up Ballona's creeks into the pickleweed, and set the calendar for the grunion, which spawn on the beach on the nights just after the highest tides. All of that is real.

Offshore, giant kelp grows toward the sun in underwater forests. Storms tear it loose, and it washes up as the wrack line. Beach hoppers and kelp flies feed on the wrack, and snowy plovers and other shorebirds feed on them. That is why a raked-clean beach is a hungrier beach.

Two kinds of weather take turns. In late spring and summer the marine layer, a low gray cloud off the cold ocean, keeps the dunes and gardens cool and damp in the morning before the sun burns it off. In fall and winter the Santa Ana winds blow hot and dry from the deserts toward the sea, drying the bluff scrub, raising fire danger and holding up the faces of the waves.

Behind the beach, the old El Segundo dunes hold the sand-loving plants that real local wildlife needs. The endangered El Segundo Blue butterfly lives almost its whole life on seacliff buckwheat, so every buckwheat planted at the preserve is a vote for next summer's butterflies. Main Street gardens help too: aloe, coral trees and bird of paradise feed hummingbirds and pollinators. Ballona Creek carries the city's runoff to the bay at Playa del Rey, which is why what goes down a storm drain ends up in the marsh.

Then there is the part that is pure fiction. In the comic, the Lantern sends its morning edition at 6:45, and the invented life of this guide tunes to it: signal sand hums, transistor tulips turn to face it, tide-clock barnacles open on cue, and beacon moss glows brighter when the station is on the air.
