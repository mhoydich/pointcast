01 / FIELD GUIDE

The largest room in El Segundo

Stand at El Segundo's shoreline and the Pacific looks like one thing: a blue surface, a horizon, a succession of breaking waves. A better way to look is to separate the clocks. The waves may carry energy from a distant storm. The water level follows a tidal rhythm. The water temperature reflects heat, currents and mixing. The breeze belongs to the atmosphere. These systems meet at the beach, but a number describing one cannot stand in for all the others.

This field guide starts in Santa Monica Bay and opens outward. Its default map point is a fixed public El Segundo reference, not your device's location. The aim is to make the ocean legible: what the instruments measure, how the coast works, and how to ask a question you can actually investigate.

02 / FIELD GUIDE

A bay inside an ocean

The Pacific is Earth's largest and deepest ocean basin. NOAA's Ocean Exploration overview puts its area above 155 million square kilometers and its average depth near 4,000 meters. Those rounded figures describe an enormous basin, not the depth beneath a particular buoy. Ocean-area totals vary with boundary conventions, so apparent disagreements in the last few million square kilometers need not be measurement failures. NOAA: Pacific scale

Our coast lies within the California Current ecosystem, a productive eastern-Pacific system connecting physical conditions to fish, seabirds and marine mammals. The view from El Segundo is therefore both local and migratory: water and organisms move through a much bigger neighborhood than a city boundary. NOAA Fisheries: California Current ecosystem

03 / FIELD GUIDE

Three instruments, three different places

A trustworthy ocean dashboard tells you where each number comes from. NOAA station 9410840 measures water level at Santa Monica, about 12.5 kilometers from this guide's El Segundo reference point. Its tide prediction is a calculation; its water-level observation is a measurement. Keep those labels even when the curves almost overlap. NOAA station information · NOAA data products

The Santa Monica Bay wave buoy, NDBC 46221 / CDIP 028, is about 21.7 kilometers from the reference point, offshore at 33.860° N, 118.641° W. Its thermometer sits approximately 0.46 meters below the waterline, over water 387 meters deep. That is offshore near-surface temperature, not a thermometer in El Segundo's breaking surf. A pier thermometer is a different measurement again. The buoy's station page identifies waves and water temperature; missing wind fields must stay missing. NOAA buoy 46221

04 / FIELD GUIDE

The tide is a moving reference line

Much of the U.S. West Coast has mixed semidiurnal tides: two highs and two lows in a lunar day, often with unequal heights. The Moon and Sun matter, but basin shape, coastlines and water movement complicate the pattern. A full Moon alone cannot tell you the time of the next local high tide. NOAA tidal cycles

Tide heights also need a datum, a defined vertical reference. This guide uses MLLW, mean lower low water: a long-term reference based on each tidal day's lower low. A negative tide height is below that reference, not a negative ocean depth. Comparing observed and predicted levels is useful only when station, timestamp, units and datum agree. NOAA tidal datums

05 / FIELD GUIDE

A wave number is not a surf report

Significant wave height summarizes the sea state. In NDBC's spectral calculation it is approximately the average height of the highest third of waves. It is neither the tallest possible wave nor the height that will break at your beach. Dominant period describes the wave-energy peak; direction describes where those waves come from. NDBC wave calculations · NDBC measurement guide

The useful question is not simply, “How many feet?” It is, “Measured where, during which interval, and describing what?” A buoy can reveal a long-period swell while leaving unanswered how that swell interacts with the local shore. That limitation makes the measurement more useful, because it stops us asking it to do a job it cannot do.

06 / FIELD GUIDE

The food web below the surface

Coastal upwelling brings deeper, nutrient-bearing water toward the surface. In the California Current system, that supply supports plankton production and the food web above it. The timing matters: a productive pulse is a sequence of physical movement, biological growth and feeding, rather than a permanent green conveyor belt. NOAA: upwelling and oceanography

Kelp forests add another architecture. These are large brown algae, not underwater trees, although their canopies offer shelter and food. They depend on light and suitable shallow, nutrient-rich water. NOAA notes that kelp can grow as much as 18 inches in a day under ideal conditions. That maximum is a biological possibility, not a promise about every local patch. NOAA: kelp forests

07 / FIELD GUIDE

The coast has a history on land

The LAX Dunes preserve, just north of El Segundo, protects roughly 307 acres of coastal dunes. LAWA began restoration in the 1980s; the habitat includes the endangered El Segundo blue butterfly. The preserve makes a quiet point: the Pacific's living edge continues beyond the wet sand. Wind, plants, dunes and development belong in an ocean story too. Use designated access and organized restoration opportunities rather than entering protected habitat. LAWA: natural resources

The city's industrial history also grew around ocean access. El Segundo's refinery was built in 1911, and the city incorporated in 1917. Reading that history beside the dunes changes the scene: the coast is simultaneously habitat, workplace, infrastructure and public space. El Segundo Library: city history

08 / FIELD GUIDE

The Pacific is a human geography

A cultural map should not begin with modern industry. Tongva/Gabrieleno communities' regional history includes Los Angeles and the Southern Channel Islands; the National Park Service describes extensive coastal and island exchange. These are particular peoples and places, not a generic “ocean culture.” National Park Service: regional maritime history

Across the Pacific, Marshallese navigation offers another way of reading waves. Swells interacting with islands produce patterns experienced navigators learn to interpret. NOAA's account shows observation as a practiced cultural knowledge system, connecting sea conditions to journeys rather than treating the ocean as empty space between destinations. NOAA Ocean Today: Marshallese navigation

09 / FIELD GUIDE

Work, climate and the cost of a healthy coast

The ocean economy includes recreation, transportation, fishing, construction, research and more. NOAA and BEA estimate that the U.S. marine economy supported 2.6 million jobs and contributed $511 billion to GDP in 2023. Those are national figures covering oceans and the Great Lakes, not El Segundo's output or the Pacific alone. NOAA marine economy

The ocean also absorbs atmospheric carbon dioxide. The resulting chemistry lowers pH and reduces the availability of carbonate used by shell-building organisms. “Acidification” describes a shift toward lower pH; it does not require seawater to cross below pH 7. This connects the sea outside town to choices and processes across the planet. NOAA ocean acidification

10 / FIELD GUIDE

Read the ocean; respect its limits

No tide, temperature or buoy card certifies swimming conditions. Check current LA County water-quality advisories and the local beach forecast, and follow lifeguards and posted closures. The county recommends avoiding ocean-water contact for three days after significant rain, especially near flowing outlets; an advisory can last longer. NWS warns that sunny, calm-looking days can still have dangerous rip currents. An honest guide helps you notice those separate questions. LA County beach water quality · NWS rip-current safety

11 / FIELD GUIDE

Supplement: pocket glossary

  • Tide: the recurring rise and fall associated with astronomical forcing, expressed locally through an ocean basin.
  • Observed water level: the level measured at a named station and time, including influences beyond the predicted tide.
  • MLLW: mean lower low water, the reference used for this guide's tide heights.
  • Swell: waves that have traveled beyond their generating wind system.
  • Significant wave height: a statistical sea-state measure, approximately the highest-third mean, not a maximum.
  • Dominant period: the period associated with the largest peak in the wave-energy spectrum.
  • Upwelling: movement bringing deeper water toward the surface.
  • Datum: the reference from which a height is measured.

13 / FIELD GUIDE

University of El Segundo: three short exercises

1. Read a number's passport · 10 minutes

Choose one tide card and one buoy card. Write the source, station, location, observation or prediction time, unit, and measurement type. Circle anything missing. Success: you can explain why offshore temperature is not a beach-temperature claim.

2. Predict, then inspect · 20 minutes

Open one complete day of Santa Monica predictions. Mark each high and low and compare the two highs. Ask whether the day shows a mixed pattern. Success: a labeled sketch with date, time zone and MLLW, plus one sentence about why a lunar phase is insufficient to predict a local high-tide time.

3. Audit a surf sentence · 10 minutes

Rewrite this deliberately misleading claim: “The buoy says three feet, so the waves at El Segundo are three feet and safe.” Success: your correction distinguishes offshore significant wave height, beach breakers and independently assessed hazards. No water entry is part of this exercise.

14 / FIELD GUIDE

University of El Segundo: three practical projects

A seven-day ocean notebook · free, from home or dry land

Record one comparable tide prediction and one timestamped buoy observation daily for seven days. Keep gaps as gaps. Add a question each day rather than guessing a cause. Deliverable: two small charts, a source log, and a paragraph explaining what the data cannot tell you. Do not scrape faster than a source updates.

A coast-as-system map · paper or a mapping tool

Mark the public reference point, Santa Monica station, offshore buoy, dunes, and beach-advisory link. Draw separate arrows for information flow, water movement and a possible runoff pathway; label unverified pathways as questions. Deliverable: a map legend that distinguishes instruments, ecosystems and infrastructure. No exact personal location is needed.

A public-data quality checker · beginner coding

Build a reader for the buoy's whitespace text feed. Use column names; convert MM to missing; parse the timestamp as UTC; retain original units. Add a “last observation” label, a stale state, and a source link. Test with a deliberately old row, an empty file and a missing thermometer value. Deliverable: a small visualization that never silently turns an absent reading into zero.
