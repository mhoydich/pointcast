What happens when the bot is still working after the conversation ends?

That is the part of this wave that interests PointCast. A good answer is useful. A finished piece of work, waiting in the right place, could change how a small operation runs.

The possibilities are easy to imagine. An old creative archive becomes a story worth revisiting. Research becomes a draft, then a visual treatment, then something ready to review. A local project gets a useful page instead of another abandoned planning document. There is a lot of work between having an idea and putting it into the world.

This is an exploration from a builder’s perspective, without trying to crown a winner after a perfect laboratory test. It maps current products and the questions worth pushing them on. Product descriptions come from public documentation reviewed on October 27, 2026. They do not establish that PointCast has personally verified every feature or given every bot the same assignment.

## A crowded room, very quickly

Several big names have arrived in a tight stretch: Grok Bot was announced on August 11, OpenAI’s dots on September 29, and Claude Duet on October 21. Those are announcement dates, with different access conditions and levels of maturity. [Grok Bot](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot), [dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/), [Duet](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet).

Underneath the different names, there is a shared ambition: give the AI somewhere to work, enough context to be useful, and a way to return when the next decision belongs to a person. That is an interpretation of the product direction; these systems are not equivalent.

The interesting differences are in the arrangements. Who owns the workspace? How does the bot learn what matters? Can you watch it work? What stops it? What happens when the work gets expensive?

### Grok Bot: the small-team idea

Grok Bot is the agent product, distinct from an ordinary Grok chat. Its public material emphasizes persistent cloud computing, multiple Bots, shared context, and routines learned from demonstrated work. The launch describes a beta, with subscription eligibility and a separate enterprise waitlist. Its proposition is closer to a little operating team than a single question-and-answer window. [Grok Bot launch](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot), [Grok product FAQ](https://docs.x.ai/grok/faq).

That is an appealing arrangement when several projects are moving at once. Research, production, and checking could happen without one person carrying notes between every conversation.

But adding more agents raises another question: who is responsible for the final result? Three Bots agreeing with one another would not prove that a page works or a source is right. There still needs to be one clear owner and evidence someone can inspect.

### Dots: continuity as the product

OpenAI describes dots as always-on agents with their own cloud computer, connected apps, and preferences learned through feedback. Its current help page describes a gradual rollout, including eligible adult Pro users outside the EEA, Switzerland, and UK, Business Premium users in supported regions, and admin-enabled Enterprise beta access. Account and workspace requirements still apply. [Introducing dots](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/), [getting started](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001530-getting-started-with-your-dot).

The attraction is continuity. A correction to a headline, an explanation of the audience, or a change in direction should survive the next task.

“Proactive” also deserves a closer look. OpenAI’s launch documentation says its background proactive research uses read-only tools. Finding something useful and being authorized to change something are separate steps. That boundary is worth understanding before handing over a real workflow. [Dots safeguards](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/).

### Duet: attention becomes part of the job

Claude Duet is the newest of this group. Anthropic opened its research preview through a waitlist on October 21, initially for US Pro and Max subscribers. It describes a background agent that prioritizes connected information and brings decisions back to the user. The announcement says it checks connected accounts every few hours to conserve the same usage allowance used by the rest of Claude. [Introducing Claude Duet](https://claude.com/resources/articles/claude-duet).

That last detail is refreshingly concrete. “Always on” does not have to mean checking every service every second. There is a practical design problem here: how often should a bot look, how much should it spend, and when is an interruption worth it?

The interesting test is whether this kind of assistant can make a day feel clearer. An impressive volume of activity would not be enough if the evening then went to sorting through it.

## The rest of the field is not background noise

### Kimi: choose the place

Kimi deserves more precise language than a single entry on a chatbot list. Moonshot’s documentation separates its online Agent from Kimi Work, a local desktop agent for Mac and Windows, and Kimi Claw, an always-online cloud offering. Kimi Work launched June 3 and remains beta in the current documentation. [Kimi Agent overview](https://www.kimi.com/en/help/agent/agent-overview), [Kimi Work overview](https://www.kimi.com/en/help/kimi-work/overview).

That raises an important choice: help inside a person’s own working computer, or a separate place where the agent can keep going? Those arrangements bring different permissions, failure modes, and expectations. It makes sense to choose around the job before choosing around the model name.

### Manus and Cue: two doors

Manus has also become more than one product. Its September 28 release introduced Manus 2.0, with creation tools, cloud computers, and event-triggered automations. It also introduced **Cue**, a separate personal-agent app. The launch page describes Manus 2.0 as available and Cue as invite-code early access. Those should not be collapsed into the same access claim. [Manus 2.0 and Cue](https://manus.im/blog/introducing-manus-2-0).

### Zo: a computer to come back to

Zo’s appeal is the workspace itself. Zo Computer advertises a personal cloud computer for building and hosting projects, with an agent and options to connect model providers. Its public page also spells out that trial computers sleep when idle and that ordinary access ends without a paid plan. The infrastructure terms matter alongside the AI. [Zo Computer](https://www.zo.computer/).

### Instinct: start with a message

Instinct comes in from the other direction: as little new interface as possible. Its public site presents an assistant you text or call that can use a phone and computer. The page gives examples including groceries and travel, but does not establish general access or detailed pricing. Those questions remain open. [Instinct](https://instinct.com/).

These are different bets on the same everyday problem: too many things need doing, and the person with the idea is often also the entire operations department.

## What is actually worth measuring

For the next round of experiments, five questions seem more useful than a wall of feature checkmarks.

**Does it keep the thread?** After a day away, can it explain what changed, what is finished, and what is waiting? A long memory is useful only if it carries the right context forward.

**Does it understand permission?** Drafting, editing, publishing, purchasing, and deleting are different levels of authority. Those differences should be visible. A reviewable draft is often exactly the right result.

**Can it prove completion?** For a website, that means the actual page opened and checked. For writing, sources and a finished draft that can be found again. For code, the relevant tests and changed behavior inspected. “Done” needs something attached to it.

**Can it stay useful inside a budget?** It should be clear when a job needs more time or compute, and whether a simpler approach would work. A bot that quietly burns through an allowance can turn a small experiment into another management problem.

**Does it reduce supervision?** An hour diagnosing access, repeating context, or locating output belongs in the result. The useful unit is the finished job and the human effort it took to get there.

A modest, repeatable assignment would be a useful next step: research one PointCast story. Prepare the draft and visual brief. Preserve the sources. Stop before publishing. Report what worked, what failed, what needed approval, and what it cost in time and usage.

That is a proposed experiment. There are no comparative trial results or rankings to report here yet.

## Still early, worth exploring

The work worth making already exists in pieces: ideas, archives, conversations, half-built projects. Better follow-through could make more of it real.

The flashy part of this moment is the growing cast of Bots, dots, and agents. The part worth watching is quieter: the task that comes back finished, the boundary that holds, the correction that sticks, the project that moves forward without needing another afternoon of supervision.

That is the progress PointCast wants to explore.
